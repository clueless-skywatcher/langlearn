The Verbs, Present Tense and modes of addressing

1. A verb is something that signifies an action. E.g. Der Mann isst einen Apfel. Here "isst" is the verb.
[The accusative "einen Apfel" is section 07. Fine as incidental text, but no rule here should lean on it — and "isst" is a strong verb (see 12), so it is not a good specimen for the regular pattern.]

2. The Present tense of a verb signifies an action that is happening NOW, at the current time.

3. German present tense does not have separate forms for simple or continuous tense, i.e. you use the same verb form for present tense, even if it is a habitual action (e.g. he goes) or a continuous one (e.g. he is going). E.g. Der Mann isst einen Apfel - Can mean both "The man is eating an apple" and "The man eats an apple".

4. Verbs in German are conjugated by tense, person and number. First person refers to the speaker themselves, second person refers to the direct listener and third person refers to someone who is neither the speaker or the listener.
E.g.
- Ich esse Fleisch.
- Wir gehen jetzt.
- Du singst gut.
- Ihr singt wirklich schlecht.
- Er geht nach Hause.
- Sie singen laut.
[Two of your examples were changed. "Er geht zu Hause" -> "Er geht nach Hause": "zu Hause" is static, "at home", and gehen is motion, so it takes "nach Hause". "Er ist zu Hause" would be the static one. "Sie koennen da tun" is not grammatical (it wants an object: "Sie koennen das tun"), and koennen is a modal, which is section 11 — replaced with a plain verb.]

5. The personal pronouns in the nominative are:

    singular            plural
 1  ich                 wir
 2  du                  ihr
 3  er / sie / es       sie

and beside them the polite Sie, which is the same word as the third person plural and is written with a capital S wherever it stands.

6. Verbs are classified into two types - regular and irregular. A regular verb follows a fixed pattern to build its conjugation. An irregular verb does not follow it, but the departure is almost always in the stem rather than in the endings: the six endings of 7 are the same for nearly every German verb, and what changes is the vowel or the shape of the stem they are attached to. Of the verbs in this section only sein departs from the endings themselves; the modal verbs of section 11 do so as well.
E.g. The conjugation of kaufen is: kaufe, kaufst, kauft, kaufen, kauft, kaufen. But the conjugation of sein is: bin, bist, ist, sind, seid, sind.
[Your original wording was "irregular verbs don't have a fixed pattern for conjugation". That is too strong and it hides what a learner actually has to watch for. fahren is irregular, yet its endings are exactly kaufen's — only the vowel of the stem moves, du faehrst. See 12.]

7. A regular verb usually has an -en or -n at the end of its infinitive form. To conjugate that: Remove that -en/-n to obtain the stem, then append these suffixes: -e, -st, -t, -en/-n, -t, -en/-n.
E.g. kaufen - en = kauf- -> ich kaufe, du kaufst, er/sie/es kauft, wir kaufen, ihr kauft, sie/Sie kaufen.
[Verified — this is right, and it is the standard description. Three things qualify it, and each is a rule of its own: 8, 9 and 10. Note also that the 1st and 3rd person plural are always identical to the infinitive, which is why the -en/-n alternation in the ending simply follows whichever the infinitive had.]

8. A verb whose stem ends in -d or -t inserts an -e- before the -st and -t endings, because the ending would otherwise be unpronounceable. The same happens after -m or -n when a consonant other than l or r stands before it.
E.g.
- arbeiten -> ich arbeite, du arbeitest, er arbeitet, wir arbeiten, ihr arbeitet, sie arbeiten
- finden -> du findest, er findet, ihr findet
- atmen -> du atmest, er atmet, ihr atmet

9. A verb whose stem ends in an s-sound - -s, -ss, -x or -z - takes only -t in the second person singular, not -st, because the s of the ending has already been said.
E.g.
- heissen -> du heisst (not "heissst")
- reisen -> du reist
- sitzen -> du sitzt
- tanzen -> du tanzt
This makes the second and third persons singular identical in these verbs: du heisst, er heisst.
[Written with ss here because the notes are in ASCII; in the JSON these are heissen/heisst with the eszett and reisen with a single s.]

10. A verb whose infinitive ends in -eln or -ern has a stem in -el or -er, and takes -n rather than -en in the first and third person plural. The -e- of the stem is often dropped in the first person singular.
E.g.
- sammeln -> ich sammle, du sammelst, er sammelt, wir sammeln, ihr sammelt, sie sammeln
- aendern -> ich aendere, du aenderst, er aendert, wir aendern, ihr aendert, sie aendern

11. In German there are two modes of addressing - Du (informal) and Sie (formal). The Sie-form of addressing requires the same ending as third-person plural.
E.g.
- Was machst du? (What are you doing?)
- Was machen Sie? (What are you doing (formal)?)
The informal has a plural of its own, ihr, used to address more than one person one is on du terms with. Sie serves for one person and for many alike.
E.g. Was macht ihr? (What are you (all) doing?)

12. A group of verbs changes the vowel of the stem in the second and third person singular, and nowhere else: the first person singular, the whole plural and the infinitive keep the vowel unchanged. The endings are the ordinary ones of 7.
The changes are a -> ae, e -> i, e -> ie, and au -> aeu.
E.g.
- fahren -> ich fahre, du faehrst, er faehrt, wir fahren, ihr fahrt, sie fahren
- geben -> du gibst, er gibt
- sprechen -> du sprichst, er spricht
- lesen -> du liest, er liest
- laufen -> du laeufst, er laeuft
- essen -> du isst, er isst
[essen is where 9 and 12 meet: the vowel moves e -> i, and the stem then ends in -ss, so the second person takes -t alone. That is why "isst" of 1 and 3 does double duty for du and for er.]

13. sein is the one verb whose endings are its own, and it has to be taken whole:
ich bin, du bist, er/sie/es ist, wir sind, ihr seid, sie/Sie sind.

14. haben is regular except in the second and third person singular, which drop the -b:
ich habe, du hast, er/sie/es hat, wir haben, ihr habt, sie/Sie haben.

15. man is a pronoun that names no one in particular - English "one", "you", or "people". It takes the third person singular, like er.
E.g.
- Hier spricht man Deutsch. (German is spoken here.)
- Man isst hier gut.

16. In a German statement the conjugated verb stands second. What stands first is one element, and it need not be the subject; when something else takes first place, the subject moves in behind the verb.
E.g.
- Ich gehe heute nach Hause.
- Heute gehe ich nach Hause.
- Nach Hause gehe ich heute.
All three are the same sentence with a different first element, and in all three the verb is the second thing in the sentence.

17. sein, heissen and werden do not take an object. What follows them names the subject over again, and stands in the nominative.
E.g.
- Er heisst Heinz Bartels.
- Das ist mein Bruder.
- Er wird Arzt.

18. An adjective standing after sein, werden or bleiben takes no ending at all, whatever the gender and number of the subject.
E.g.
- Der Film ist langweilig.
- Die Filme sind langweilig.
- Das Buch ist neu.
- Die Kinder sind klein.
[The endings an adjective takes in front of its noun - ein moderner Film - are A2 and are on the outline's "Not at A1" list, so this rule has to stay on the predicative side and say nothing about the attributive one.]
