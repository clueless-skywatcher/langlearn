The Accusative

1. German marks the role a noun plays in the sentence by its case. The
accusative is the case of the direct object - the thing or person the action of
the verb lands on. The inventory calls it the Akkusativergänzung.
E.g.
- Ich kaufe den Apfel.       (the apple is what is bought)
- Der Mann liest das Buch.   (the book is what is read)
- Wir suchen die Frau.       (the woman is who is sought)

2. In nearly every noun the case is carried by the article and not by the noun.
The accusative forms of the definite article are:

              nominative      accusative
 masculine    der Mann        den Mann
 feminine     die Frau        die Frau
 neuter       das Kind        das Kind
 plural       die Kinder      die Kinder

3. So the masculine singular is the only place where the accusative differs
from the nominative at all: der -> den. Feminine, neuter and plural are
identical in the two cases.
E.g.
- Der Mann kauft den Stuhl.   (masculine: der -> den)
- Der Mann kauft die Katze.   (feminine: unchanged)
- Der Mann kauft das Auto.    (neuter: unchanged)
- Der Mann kauft die Bücher.  (plural: unchanged)

4. The indefinite article and kein follow the same pattern, and again only the
masculine singular moves.

              nominative      accusative
 masculine    ein Mann        einen Mann
 feminine     eine Frau       eine Frau
 neuter       ein Kind        ein Kind
 plural       (Kinder)        (Kinder)

 masculine    kein Mann       keinen Mann
 feminine     keine Frau      keine Frau
 neuter       kein Kind       kein Kind
 plural       keine Kinder    keine Kinder

E.g. Ich kaufe einen Apfel. / Ich kaufe keinen Apfel.
[kein and its declension are section 08 in the outline, which follows this one
precisely so that keinen can be stated. Listing the forms here is what makes 08
a one-line section; whether the table lives here or there is a call for whoever
authors 08.]

5. One group of masculine nouns changes the noun as well as the article: the
weak masculines, which add -n or -en in every case but the nominative
singular.
E.g.
- der Student -> den Studenten
- der Mensch  -> den Menschen
- der Junge   -> den Jungen
- der Nachbar -> den Nachbarn
- der Herr    -> den Herrn        (plural die Herren)
[Student and Nachbar are both already in the course - Student in the section 04
vocabulary, Nachbarn in the checkpoint 1 passage - so the learner has met the
plural -en without being told why it is there. This is a real inventory
question: the Grammatik-Inventar names the Kasus but no row for the weak
declension. It is needed to state the accusative of Student correctly under §14
of the authoring rules, so my reading is that it belongs here as a fact about
those nouns rather than as a declension class to be drilled.]

6. The personal pronouns have their own accusative forms, and here every person
changes, not only the masculine.

              nominative      accusative
 1 sg         ich             mich
 2 sg         du              dich
 3 sg m       er              ihn
 3 sg f       sie             sie
 3 sg n       es              es
 1 pl         wir             uns
 2 pl         ihr             euch
 3 pl         sie             sie
 polite       Sie             Sie

E.g.
- Kennst du mich?
- Ich sehe ihn.
- Wir besuchen euch.
- Sie fragt uns.
[The inventory row names mich, dich, ihn, sie and es. uns and euch are on the
list too, and are needed for 8 below in any case.]

7. The interrogative pronoun has an accusative form as well. wer asks after a
person in the nominative and wen after a person in the accusative; was is the
same word in both cases (¶71).
E.g.
- Wer sieht den Mann?   (who is doing the seeing)
- Wen sieht der Mann?   (who is being seen)
- Was kaufst du?
[This pair is the sharpest test of the case there is in A1: the two questions
use the same three words and differ only in the case of the pronoun, and the
answer to one is the subject of the other.]

8. uns, euch and sich also serve as reciprocal pronouns, where two or more
people do something to each other. The inventory names sich and uns.
E.g.
- Wir sehen uns morgen.      (we are seeing each other tomorrow)
- Kennt ihr euch?            (do you know each other)
- Sie treffen sich hier.     (they are meeting each other here)
sich is the form for the third person, singular and plural alike, and for the
polite Sie.

9. Four pronouns of quantity take no ending at all and stand unchanged in the
accusative: etwas (something), nichts (nothing), alles (everything) and mehr
(more).
E.g.
- Ich kaufe etwas.
- Er sagt nichts.
- Sie weiß alles.
- Wir brauchen mehr Zeit.
welch- is the exception among them and declines like the definite article:
welchen Mann, welche Frau, welches Kind, welche Bücher.
E.g. Welchen Apfel nimmst du?
[jeder and alle as determiners, and viel/wenig, are A2 by the outline's "Not at
A1" table. Only the pronoun alles is listed, which is why 9 has alles and not
alle.]

10. Most verbs that can take an object take it in the accusative. Of the verbs
the course has already taught: haben, kaufen, machen, lernen, finden, lesen,
essen, sprechen, suchen, brauchen, nehmen, sehen, kennen, fragen, besuchen,
trinken.
E.g.
- Ich habe einen Bruder.
- Wir nehmen den Zug.
- Sie sucht das Buch.
- Er trinkt einen Kaffee.
[Bruder, kennen, fragen, nehmen, sehen, suchen, brauchen, trinken are new
words. All are Wortliste entries; they are here because a section on the
accusative with only the section 05 verbs to work with cannot show the case
doing anything.]

11. Three verbs already taught take no accusative at all, because what follows
them is not an object: sein, werden and bleiben take a complement in the
nominative (¶67).
E.g.
- Das ist ein Apfel.       (nominative - not "einen Apfel")
- Das Kind wird ein Mann.
[This is the trap worth building the hard drills on. A learner who has just
learnt den/einen will put it after ist, which is exactly the error the
nominative complement rule of section 05 forbids. With a profession it is
doubly wrong: Er ist Lehrer takes no article at all (¶45), so neither "ein
Lehrer" nor "einen Lehrer" is available there. A second group - danken,
gehören, helfen - takes the dative instead, and is section 13.]

12. es gibt takes the accusative, and is the ordinary way of saying that
something exists or is available. It does not change for number: es gibt takes
a singular or a plural object alike.
E.g.
- Es gibt hier einen Bahnhof.
- Es gibt keine Kartoffeln.
[geben is a Wortliste verb and es gibt is an A1 idiom, but the Grammatik-
Inventar gives no row for it. Flagged for a decision: it is the single most
useful accusative frame in the language and it is hard to justify leaving out,
but adding it adds to the inventory.]

13. Some prepositions govern the accusative always, whatever the sense. The
full set is durch, für, gegen, ohne, um, entlang, wider and bis.
E.g.
- für den Mann        (for the man)
- ohne das Auto       (without the car)
- um den Tisch        (around the table)
- durch die Stadt     (through the town)
- gegen die Wand      (against the wall)
- bis Montag          (until Monday)
[bis shows no ending on Montag, so that example states the government without
demonstrating it; the case only becomes visible on a masculine singular with a
determiner. Scope, and this is the part of your request that sits worst with
the outline.
Of the eight: für and ohne are A1, in section 12 with the modal prepositions.
um is A1, in section 11 with the times. bis is A1 only in the pair von ... bis,
also section 11. durch and gegen are named in the "Not at A1" table as A2 -
they are Wortliste words and may appear in a passage, but neither carries a
rule. entlang and wider are not in the A1 inventory at any point.

So a rule here that lists all eight would be adding five items to the syllabus
and taking three out of the sections they were assigned to. My recommendation
is that this section states the *fact* - that a preposition can fix the case of
what follows it, and that some fix the accusative - with für and ohne as the
two examples, and that the list proper stays in section 12 where the
prepositions live. That keeps the accusative complete as a case without
importing the preposition syllabus into it.]

14. Nine prepositions govern either case: an, auf, in, über, unter, vor,
hinter, neben and zwischen. They take the accusative for a movement towards
something and the dative for a position at it.
E.g.
- Ich gehe in die Stadt.     (accusative: motion into)
- Ich wohne in der Stadt.    (dative: position in)
- Er legt das Buch auf den Tisch.
- Das Buch liegt auf dem Tisch.
[Section 12, which the outline gives as "an and in with dative against
accusative" - two of the nine, not all nine. The other seven are not in the A1
preposition inventory. Kept here as notes because the contrast is the reason
the accusative matters beyond the direct object, but it is not this section's
material and the dative it is contrasted with is section 13 anyway.]

15. A definite stretch or point of time stands in the accusative with no
preposition at all.
E.g.
- Ich arbeite jeden Tag.
- Wir bleiben eine Woche.
- Nächsten Montag fahren wir.
[Section 11 territory, and jeden is a form of jeder, which the "Not at A1"
table puts at A2. eine Woche and nächsten Montag are usable; jeden Tag is a
fixed Wortliste phrase and can stand as vocabulary without the determiner rule
behind it.]

16. A measure - a price, a weight, a distance, an age - also stands in the
accusative.
E.g.
- Das Buch kostet einen Euro.
- Er ist einen Meter groß.
[This is why Was kostet die Fahrkarte? has was in the accusative and not the
nominative: the price is a measure, and die Fahrkarte is the subject the verb
agrees with. The de-06 drill de106-q14 already turns on that agreement, so the
fact is owed a statement somewhere.]
